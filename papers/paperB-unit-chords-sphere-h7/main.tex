\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Give the unit sphere $\Sph(1)\subset\R^3$ the graph structure in which two
points are adjacent when their Euclidean distance --- their chord, not their
geodesic --- is $1$. Write $h$ for the largest number of points whose chord-$1$
graph has independence number at most $2$; in the notation of Bachoc, Bekker,
Moustrou and de Oliveira Filho this is $\indep(3,\tfrac12)$, since a unit chord
means inner product $\tfrac12$. We prove
\[
h=7,
\]
and that the extremal configuration is unique up to isomorphism.

The lower bound is not new, though it does not appear in this form: Theorem 5.2
of Bachoc et al.\ shows that the $2$-Moser spindle $\mathrm{MS}(2)$ is
$(3,t)$-realizable for $t\ge t_{2,3}=0.2553\ldots$, and $\tfrac12$ exceeds that
threshold. What we add is the identification --- the extremal configuration
\emph{is} the Moser spindle, with its $7$ vertices, $11$ edges and degree
sequence $[3,3,3,3,3,3,4]$ --- together with an exact realisation of its Gram
matrix over $\Q(\sqrt{46})$.

The contribution is the \textbf{upper bound and the uniqueness}. The
semidefinite and spectral bounds of Bachoc et al.\ are established for
$t\in[-1,0]$, and they note explicitly that the bound $2(n+1)$ does not extend
to positive $t$, the Larman--Rogers configuration giving $\indep(5,\tfrac15)\ge16$
against $2(n+1)=12$. The value $t=\tfrac12$ therefore lies outside every bound
in the literature. We show $h\le7$ by an argument that is short and specific to
this inner product: the chord-$1$ graph is $K_4$-free, since four mutually
adjacent unit vectors would have Gram matrix $\tfrac12(I+J)$, which is positive
definite; with independence at most $2$ giving a triangle-free complement,
$R(3,4)=9$ yields $h\le8$. Since the $(3,4)$-Ramsey graphs on eight vertices are
\emph{unique} up to isomorphism, exactly one combinatorial candidate remains,
and its exclusion reduces --- via a triangle-basis elimination in which
non-edges are left entirely unconstrained --- to a single polynomial system
whose Gr\"obner basis is $\{1\}$.

We also record two structural facts about the spherical family. The vertex link
is described in closed form, classifying the radii at which it closes into a
cycle; and at radius exactly $1$ it never closes, by Niven's theorem, since the
relevant cosine is $1/3$. The second closes the cone route
$\chrom(\R^3)\ge1+\chrom(\Sph(1))$ by a number-theoretic rather than geometric
obstruction, and explains why densifying spherical graphs at radius $1$ leaves
the chromatic number at $4$ in every experiment: there is no primitive gadget to
begin with.
\end{abstract}
